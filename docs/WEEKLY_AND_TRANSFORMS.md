## 1. The skew fix, replacing a policy

The README said: *"the skew workaround is a policy, not a fix… the right answer for a skewed characteristic is a transformation or a distribution-appropriate chart, and neither is implemented."* Both are now. `seal_force_N` has skew parameter 0.55, sample skew **1.42**.

| fit | skew after | Anderson–Darling | normal at 5%? |
|---|---|---|---|
| none (raw) | 1.42 | 21.186 | **no** |
| Box-Cox (λ = 0.126) | 0.01 | 0.484 | **yes** |
| Johnson S<sub>U</sub> | -0.02 | 0.343 | **yes** |

**The test after the transformation is the point.** A transformation is a hypothesis — *this map makes the data normal* — and shipping one without re-testing is exactly the error it was supposed to fix.

*A note on the fit itself:* Box-Cox is location-sensitive, and this data spans 170–216 — a max/min ratio of 1.27, over which x^λ is nearly linear whatever λ is. Fitting it unshifted returned **λ = −13.4** and destroyed the data through catastrophic cancellation. Shifting the minimum to near zero (the two-parameter Box-Cox, shift = 168.0) makes λ identifiable: 0.126, essentially a log.

### What the three approaches say about the same data

The three disagree, and the disagreement is the size of the problem. Normal theory is the one that is wrong here and it is also the one with the familiar name.

**And the transformed control limits are asymmetric about the centre line** — 170.1 / 178.8 / 210.4 in original units, a ratio of 3.62. That is correct and it looks broken to anybody taught that control limits are symmetric. It is a training problem rather than a statistical one, and it is the usual reason transformations get abandoned in practice.

### The real argument for transforming

Runs-rule violations on the **raw** data: **129**. On the **transformed** data: **129**.

Pass 3 handled the skew by judging stability on rule 1 alone, because rules 2–8 assume symmetry and fire on the shape rather than the process. That was defensible and it threw away seven rules. On transformed data the symmetry is restored, so **the full rule set becomes valid again** — which is what a policy could never deliver.

### The guard fired on my own mistake

The first version of this ran `simulate_study()` with its **defaults** — a generic study at σ_part = 1.0 — and applied its GRR (σ = 0.132) to a roughness measured in micrometres with σ_observed = 0.087. The two had nothing to do with each other, and the correction refused:

> gauge variance exceeds observed variance -- the gauge study and the process study are inconsistent, and neither Cpk is trustworthy until that is resolved

That is a better demonstration than any test I could have written for it. The obvious alternative implementation — clamp the negative variance to zero and carry on — would have reported a plausible corrected Cpk built on a gauge study of a different characteristic, and nothing would ever have flagged it.

**Ageing is the single most useful thing a disposition queue does, and it is impossible without persistence.** An in-process queue is born every morning: it cannot tell a three-week-old item from one raised yesterday, and an item nobody has touched in three weeks is a different problem from the same item raised yesterday.

The UNIQUE key on (characteristic, subgroup, rule) is what makes the weekly job idempotent. Without it, re-running the analysis on Monday raises a second event for every excursion already being worked, and the queue is unreadable within a month.
